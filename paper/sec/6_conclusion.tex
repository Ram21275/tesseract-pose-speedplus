\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Ground-truth crops.} Most reported results use ground-truth boxes. On SPEED+, a synthetic-only detector roughly doubles the rotation error on lightbox ($11.2^\circ\to23.9^\circ$) and raises the score from $0.227$ to $0.523$, mainly through poor real-domain boxes and the box-relative translation target (\cref{tab:spp_sota}). A deployable system therefore needs a detector that transfers to real images, or translation that does not depend on the box.
\item \textbf{Translation on general objects.} The translation block is evaluated on the spacecraft application; its evaluation on T-LESS, SYMSOL-T and YCB-V is running (\cref{tab:trans_xobj}), so these datasets are currently compared on rotation only.
\item \textbf{How general the shared design is.} The backbone, head architecture, recipe and inference algorithm are shared, but two options are chosen per application on validation data: orbit voting helps on T-LESS, SYMSOL-T and SPEED+ but hurts on YCB-V, and MoGe-2 fusion does not help on every dataset. YCB-V also needed synthetic training data. Four domains are evidence for, not proof of, the claim that one backbone and one algorithm serve many applications.
\item \textbf{Matched comparisons.} Published numbers use different crops, masks, object sets and fine-tuned backbones; \cref{tab:tless_sota,tab:symsol_sota,tab:spp_sota} state each setting, and only rows with the same setting are matched.
\item \textbf{Hierarchy vs.\ grid.} The flat-baseline comparison of \cref{sec:abl_flat} uses a coarser grid than the tree; the same-grid control is still running, so the share of the gain due to hierarchical prediction is not yet established.
\item \textbf{Quantisation floor.} The depth-5 leaf quantisation ($2.51^\circ$ mean) bounds fine accuracy for asymmetric objects. Our best synthetic SPEED+ errors ($3.3$--$3.7^\circ$) are close to it, and it caps fine-threshold recall on T-LESS. A continuous refinement on top of the selected mode is a natural next step; the refiners we tried helped only on synthetic data (\cref{sec:supp_negative}).
\item \textbf{Per-object heads.} One head is trained per object; there is no zero-shot transfer to new objects (\cref{sec:supp_zeroshot}). Adding an object means training a new head (minutes on one GPU), not fine-tuning the backbone.
\item \textbf{Cost.} Orbit voting costs $4\times$ inference, and MoGe-2 fusion does not help on every dataset.
\item \textbf{Timing.} Timings come from a shared GPU and are upper bounds.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
One frozen foundation backbone and one core algorithm, specialised to each application by a small trained head, are a competitive basis for 6D object pose when the head's hypotheses are \emph{selected} well. Three components target selection: multi-path targets that spread the training signal over all symmetric equivalents, orbit voting that scores hierarchical hypotheses for consistency across exactly mapped in-plane rotations, and orbit distillation that moves this consistency into single-view students. Multi-path targets improve T-LESS at every recall threshold and every SYMSOL-T shape and, unlike canonical labels, remain compatible with orbit voting. Synthetic training data turns YCB-V from a failure into a working model. The same design carries over unchanged to spacecraft pose under a synthetic-to-real shift, where orbit voting and distillation repair most real-domain flips of the nearly symmetric target. Fine-tuned methods remain more accurate on all four benchmarks, and our evaluation is intentionally conservative; we hope the protocol, the matched comparisons and the negative results are as useful as the method.
