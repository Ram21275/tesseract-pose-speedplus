\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Ground-truth crops.} All benchmark results except the SPEED+ detector row use ground-truth boxes. On SPEED+, a synthetic-only detector more than doubles the rotation error on lightbox ($11.21^\circ\to23.88^\circ$) and raises the score from $0.227$ to $0.523$, mainly through poor real-domain boxes and the box-relative translation target (\cref{tab:spp_sota}). A deployable system therefore needs a detector that transfers to real images, or translation that does not depend on the box. Detection was not evaluated on the general-object benchmarks.
\item \textbf{Translation for the allocentric conversion.} On T-LESS and YCB-V the rotation is predicted in the allocentric frame and needs a translation for the conversion. Using the predicted instead of the ground-truth translation changes the rotation error by at most $0.01^\circ$, but this was measured on the T-LESS heads before distillation, because the translation block is attached to that tree; the distilled T-LESS students were evaluated with the ground-truth translation only.
\item \textbf{How general the shared architecture is.} The backbones, feature interface, head architecture, recipe and inference algorithm are shared, but each object needs its own labelled training images, intrinsics, crops and symmetry set, and its own heads; heads do not transfer zero-shot to new objects (\cref{sec:supp_zeroshot}). Two options are chosen per application on validation data: orbit aggregation helps on T-LESS, SYMSOL-T and SPEED+ but hurts on YCB-V, and MoGe-2 fusion helps only on SPEED+ and T-LESS. YCB-V also needed synthetic training data. Four applications are evidence for, not proof of, the claim that frozen backbones with small heads serve many applications; the industrial, warehouse and robotic settings named in the introduction were not evaluated.
\item \textbf{Matched comparisons.} Published numbers use different crops, masks, object sets and fine-tuned backbones; \cref{tab:tless_sota,tab:symsol_sota,tab:spp_sota,tab:trans_xobj} state each setting, and only rows with the same setting are matched.
\item \textbf{Quantisation floor.} The depth-5 leaf quantisation ($2.51^\circ$ mean) bounds fine accuracy for asymmetric objects. Our best synthetic SPEED+ errors ($3.3$--$3.7^\circ$) are close to it, and it caps fine-threshold recall on T-LESS. A continuous refinement on top of the selected mode is a natural next step; the refiners we tried helped only on synthetic data (\cref{sec:supp_negative}).
\item \textbf{Cost.} The heads are small, but the system is not: the two backbones hold 634\,M parameters, feature extraction is the dominant adaptation cost, orbit aggregation multiplies inference cost by about 4.5, and the geometry branch by about 4. Reduced precision does not speed up the head at batch 1, because the autoregressive decoding is limited by kernel launches rather than arithmetic. Timings come from a shared, heavily loaded GPU and are upper bounds.
\item \textbf{Distillation} helps on SPEED+ and T-LESS (validation $8.28^\circ\to7.88^\circ$ with orbit aggregation), but on SYMSOL-T it improves the single view only slightly ($2.54^\circ\to2.52^\circ$) and makes the orbit system worse ($2.07^\circ\to2.16^\circ$), so it is selected per application on validation data.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Shared frozen appearance and geometry backbones can support 6D pose estimation of very different known objects through compact per-object heads. The same architecture, trained only in its 2.1--2.4\,M-parameter rotation trees and 0.33\,M-parameter translation block, reaches a recall of 80\% at $10^\circ$ and 88\% at 5\,cm on real T-LESS images, $2.07^\circ$ on SYMSOL-T, $12.9^\circ$ and 1.6\,cm on YCB-V, and $11.2^\circ/11.1^\circ$ on real spacecraft images after training on renders only. The decisive problem for such heads is selecting the right mode among symmetric or look-alike rotations. Multi-path targets address it for exact symmetries on every benchmark at no inference cost, and orbit aggregation and distillation address look-alike flips where aggregation helps, at a measured $4\text{--}5\times$ inference cost. The shared design has clear boundaries: the expensive options (geometry fusion, aggregation) pay off only on some applications, cluttered household objects need synthetic training data, heads do not transfer zero-shot, and deployment on real spacecraft images is currently limited by detection rather than by the heads. Fine-tuned state-of-the-art methods remain more accurate on every benchmark we use, because they adapt the backbone features to each target; our results show how much of that benefit can be recovered without any backbone gradient, at a small fraction of the adaptation cost and with backbones that stay shared. We report validation-selected results, matched comparisons and negative results, so that the cost and the limits of adapting frozen backbones with small heads can be judged directly.
