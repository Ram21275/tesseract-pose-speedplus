\begin{abstract}
Monocular spacecraft pose estimators trained on synthetic renders lose much of their accuracy on real hardware-in-the-loop images. We study this gap on SPEED+ with a deliberately lightweight design: two frozen foundation backbones (DINOv3 for appearance, MoGe-2 for surface geometry) feed small autoregressive Transformers that predict a rotation as a root-to-leaf path in a hierarchical tesseract partition of $SO(3)$, and the two branches are fused per tree level by a training-free product of experts.
An audit of the remaining real-domain errors shows that they are dominated by $180^\circ$ flips about body axes of a nearly symmetric spacecraft, and that the correct mode is usually already among the predictor's hypotheses: the problem is \emph{selection}, not representation.
We resolve most of it with \emph{orbit voting}: the crop is rotated in-plane four times, every view's hypotheses are mapped back through an exactly derived camera-roll transform, and candidates are scored by their tree likelihood in all views. Combined with photometric augmentation, this reduces the mean rotation error from $26.9^\circ/36.7^\circ$ to $12.3^\circ/12.3^\circ$ on the lightbox/sunlamp domains while training only 2.4\,M parameters per branch. Distilling the orbit system into single-view students further lowers it to $11.2^\circ/11.1^\circ$, and a translation block on the same tokens extends the model to 6-DoF without hurting rotation.
In pre-registered ablations, the tesseract tree is far more accurate than a count-matched flat classifier, and photometric augmentation, not rotation augmentation, carries the real-domain gain. Orbit voting also transfers to T-LESS and SYMSOL-T, although absolute accuracy there remains below fine-tuned state-of-the-art methods. We report negative results throughout.
\end{abstract}
